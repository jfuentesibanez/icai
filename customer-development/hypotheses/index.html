Step 2 · What makes a good hypothesis

An assumption is a belief.
A hypothesis is a bet you can lose.

Assumption

I think my customer spends too much on gas

Hypotheses

I think 4 in 10 of my customers struggle to pay for gas by the end of the month

I think 6 in 10 would take a detour to save money on fuel

Same idea. Now it has a who, a number and a way to be wrong.

Rule 1 of 4 · Case: home-cooked meals for students away from home

Clear and simple

“I think students want our app.”

Fix. Which students? What does “want” look like? It is about our product, not their problem.

“I think students hate cooking and would love to eat healthier.”

Fix. “Hate”, “love” and “healthier” mean something different to each person. And it is two ideas in one.

“I think students away from home have sub-optimal nutritional patterns.”

Fix. Jargon sounds smart and hides the idea. Say what you would see.

“I think most students living away from home in Madrid eat fewer than 3 home-cooked dinners a week.”

Pass. One idea, plain words, something anyone can observe.

Rule 2 of 4 · Same case

Measurable

“I think a lot of students order food delivery.”

Fix. “A lot” is not a number. One student who orders makes it true.

“I think 6 in 10 students away from home order food delivery.”

Fix. How often? Once a year or every night?

“…order food delivery at least twice a week.”

Fix. How much do they spend? That is our market.

“I think 6 in 10 students away from home spend more than 40 euros a week on food delivery.”

Pass. Who, how many, how often, how much. And the 40 euros already hints at a price.

Rule 3 of 4 · Same case

Falsifiable, and testable soon

“I think we will become the go-to food brand for students in Spain.”

Fix. A dream, not a test. You would need years and the whole country to check it.

“I think students would buy healthy meals if the price were right.”

Fix. No result can prove it wrong: you can always say the price was not right yet.

“I think 2 of the 5 student residences we contact this month will agree to a 2-week pilot.”

Pass. Five emails and a month. Zero yeses means we were wrong.

“I think 20 of 100 students who see a 6-euro meal on our landing page will pre-order within a week.”

Pass. Cheap, fast, and the line between right and wrong is set in advance.

Rule 4 of 4 · Same case

Relate variables: a cause and an effect

“I think 40% of students away from home eat fast food at least 3 times a week.”

Descriptive. Useful to size the problem, but it says nothing about why or what to do.

“I think students away from home order delivery twice as often in exam weeks as in normal weeks.”

Correlational. Exam pressure (cause) linked to delivery (effect). Now we know when to sell.

“Because we believe students lose cooking time in exam weeks, if we offer a 5-day exam pack for 35 euros, we expect 1 in 5 current customers to buy it.”

Pass. Belief, action and expected result. If wrong, look at other causes: price, menu, or they just go home to their parents.

The template · copy it onto every post-it

I think that [doing or building this] for [these customers] will get [this result or KPI], and we will know it is true when [we see this metric or feedback].

Short form

Because we believe X, if we do Y, we expect Z to happen.

Step 2 · The checklist

Before a post-it goes up, check it

Is every word simple and unambiguous?

Does it say who, how many, how often, how much?

Can we test it in 1 to 4 weeks?

Can we test it with the resources we have today?

Could some evidence convince us it is wrong?

If it links two variables, is cause and effect clear?

Three kinds, all valid:

Exploratory · what we don’t know yet

Descriptive · how big, how often

Correlational · if X, then Y

Step 3 · Team work · 30 min

Board 12

Write your hypotheses

Goal: a Top 5 of hypotheses about your business model that pass the checklist.

Board 12 · The prompts

The 19 blue cards, grouped in 5 blocks

A · Problem

  • Problem, need or desire in…
  • How often it happens
  • Where they look for solutions
  • How they show it
  • What we don’t know
  • Their top 3 problems (not ours)

B · Customer

  • Who has this problem
  • Size of each segment

C · Solution

  • How it could be solved
  • Why they’ll be interested
  • Why us over competitors
  • Top 5 features
  • Time and money spent today

D · Go to market

  • How we acquire customers
  • Who decides the purchase
  • Their reasons
  • Channels to deliver value

E · Money

  • How we earn money
  • What they are willing to pay

Plus the last card: add your own questions. Your business may need prompts the board does not have.

Board 12 · Step by step

Four moves in 30 minutes

1

3 min

Split

Each person takes one or two blocks (A to E).

2

10 min

Dump

Write raw assumptions, alone and fast. One “I think…” per post-it. No filter.

3

12 min

Upgrade

As a team, turn each one into a hypothesis with the template and the 4 rules.

4

5 min

Choose

Pick the best one per block. Move your Top 5 to the top of the POST-ITS column.

House rules:

One idea per post-it

Start with “I think…”

Numbers beat adjectives

Label each post-it A to E

Board 12 · Move 3

The upgrade ladder

Start

My customer spends too much on gas

+ Who?

my customers who drive to work

+ How many?

4 in 10 of them

+ What we see?

struggle to pay for gas

+ When?

by the end of the month

+ Proof?

we will know when 4 of 10 interviewees say so

Result

I think 4 in 10 of my customers who drive to work struggle to pay for gas by the end of the month

Step 4 · Team work · 15 min

Board 14

What could go wrong?

It is October 2027. Your startup is dead. What killed it?

Board 14 · Step by step

From fears to critical hypotheses

1

Pre-mortem, alone · 4 min

Each person writes at least 3 reasons we fail. One per post-it.

2

Cluster · 3 min

Group repeated fears. Repeats are a signal.

3

Flip into hypotheses · 8 min

Write the bet the business depends on, with the 4 rules.

Example

Fear: students try us once and go back to delivery apps

I think at least 3 in 10 students who try a meal will reorder within 2 weeks

Lenses:

A rule that blocks us

A key partner who says no

Who could sabotage us

What rivals do better

Nobody pays

Step 5 · Prioritise · 10 min

Test first what could kill you and you know least about

Place every Board 14 hypothesis, plus your Board 12 Top 5, on two questions:

Criticality. If it is false, does the business model break?

Uncertainty. How little evidence do we have today?

Pick your Top 3 from the top-right corner.

↑ More uncertainty (little evidence)

Explore later

Uncertain, but we survive if it is wrong.

Test first

Could kill us and we have no evidence.

Ignore for now

Low impact, some evidence.

Monitor

Critical, but we have some evidence.

More criticality (importance in the model) →

Step 6 · On stage · 2 min per team

Your 3 main hypotheses, out loud

1

Your business

One sentence: who has the problem and what you offer.

2

Your Top 3

Read each hypothesis exactly as written on the board. No explanations.

3

Test first

Which one you would test first, and why it could kill the business.

Listen to the other teams: a risk they named may be hiding in your model too.
